Class 1 · The Verdict

Would Remi blow away a Fosse Data user today?

Yes — once she gets through the front door. And that qualifier is the whole review.

Fosse and Higham are static websites attached to paper forms, cheques and emailed PDFs. Remi's phase-aware show hub, self-completing RKC checklist, live entry countdowns, auto-numbered catalogues and in-app print ordering aren't an improvement on that world — they're a different category, and they genuinely work on a phone, which nothing else in this market does.

But the two screens where a brand-new secretary forms her first impression — the show-creation form and the show section menu — still speak developer rather than dog show. The product's soul ("guide, don't overwhelm") is fully realised in its help text and only half-realised in its structure.

"Today, Remi would blow away Mandy — and merely impress, then slightly frighten, her less-confident peers. Fix five things at the front door and it blows everyone away."

— the one-sentence summary of a full UX audit of every secretary screen

Class 2 · Already World-Class

Protect these at all costs

Fresh eyes don't just find problems. Five things in Remi are genuinely best-in-class — not "good for a small team", best-in-class full stop.

🧭 The phase-aware show hub

The show page reshapes itself around setup → entries open → show day → results, with countdowns, overdue nudges and one big phase-appropriate action. On show-day morning it becomes a single giant "Record Results" button. This is the strongest idea in the product.

✅ The self-completing checklist

An auto-seeded RKC checklist that detects completed work and ticks itself, with "Auto" badges — software that feels like a helpful assistant. No competitor has anything within a mile of this.

💬 The warmest copy in the industry

"No more bags of cash for catalogue sales… your evenings are yours again." The help-text voice is perfect for this audience — it just needs promoting from behind tiny Help buttons onto the screens themselves.

📄 The document engine

Fourteen print-quality artifacts — catalogues in five formats, schedules, prize cards, judges' books, ring boards — generated from live data. With Doxzoo fulfilment landing, this becomes a money-printing moat.

🧪 The testing culture

675 integration tests covering every journey, "every bug becomes a test first". This is why the platform survives rapid iteration. Most startups ten times Remi's size don't have this.

📱 Hard-won mobile lessons

The PDF viewer that doesn't strand iPhone users, the bottom-sheet section picker, card views instead of squashed tables — there's real evidence of watching a real secretary's thumbs. Keep doing exactly that.

Class 3 · The Big Five

Five fixes at the front door

Ranked by impact. Everything else in this report matters less than these.

1

Make creating a show three questions

Now: up to 15 visible controls on step one — including "Show Scope", "Show Ruleset" and "Class Structure", taxonomy questions a first-time secretary can't confidently answer (and which her club profile already knows).

Ask what it's called, what kind of show, and when. Create the show, fire the confetti, and land her in the Setup Wizard — which already collects everything else, with far better help text. The wizard is the good cop; let it do the interview. The current flow makes her answer everything twice.

2

Defuse the status badge

Now: the little status chip in every show header is secretly a 7-option dropdown, and only 2 of the 7 transitions ask "are you sure?". One mistap flips a live show back to Draft, silently. A permanent "Cancel Show" button sits beside it.

Status should be a read-only badge. Changing it belongs to the big guided buttons the phase panel already has ("Open entries", "Close entries", "Publish results") — with a confirm step and an undo. Power for Mandy; no landmine for everyone else.

3

One answer to "am I ready?"

Now: a draft show renders three different readiness systems at once (setup wizard, action panel, lifecycle banner), each with its own progress bar and its own "Open Entries" button — plus a fourth on the checklist page. They can't disagree, but she doesn't know that.

Pick one spine — the checklist is the natural one — and make the others views of it. One percentage, one "what's next", one place the Open Entries button lives.

4

Seven doors, not fourteen

Now: the show menu has 14 sections. "Catalogue" and "Catalogue Settings" are siblings. Money lives in both "Financial" and "Reports". The overview is labelled "Secretary Tools", which means nothing.

Merge to roughly seven: Overview · Checklist · Schedule & Classes · Entries · Money · Catalogue · People & Sponsors, with Print Shop appearing when it matters. Fourteen doors is fourteen chances to feel lost; every door removed is confidence gained.

5

A plain-English pass on the critical path

Now: the make-or-break screens say "Show Scope", "Ruleset", "Subsequent", "NFC", "Schäferhund-Verein", and the error boundary prints raw TypeError messages at a woman who will conclude the problem is her.

Write every label to a reading age of nine (GOV.UK's standard — their research shows even experts prefer it). Errors follow one formula: what happened → it's not your fault → exactly what to do next. The team already writes this way in help text; promote that voice to the labels and errors.

Class 4 · The Brand Move

Make certainty Remi's signature

The research all points one direction. Your users don't need fewer features — they need oversized targets, one stable path, plain words, and relentless confirmation that it worked.

That last number is the opportunity. Champdogs forum threads going back years show the same wound: entries failing at payment, no on-screen confirmation, fear of double charges, folk remedies like "restart your PC and type the address from scratch". Fosse never fixed it. So own it:

  • "Check your answers" before money moves. The GOV.UK pattern: one calm page summarising everything in plain English, a Change link per row, then one big button. Anxious users read confirmations — this single pattern is why government services work for this audience.
  • Over-confirm on every channel. Full-screen confirmation page (never a toast), an email that reads like a person wrote it, and a permanent green "Entered ✓" badge in My Entries. Make "did it work?" a question no Remi user has ever needed to ask — then say so on the marketing site: "You will never wonder if your entry went through."
  • Accept any format. Postcodes lowercase, phone numbers with spaces, KC numbers with or without dashes. Normalise quietly; never reject a human for formatting. Rigid validation is NN/g's #1 senior frustration.
  • Keep the furniture still. Half of older users keep written step lists; every navigation reshuffle invalidates their notes. Treat menu changes like breaking API changes — evolve screens in place. (When Fosse relaunched their site, their own users revolted on the forums.)
  • Honour your own 44px rule. The audit found the most-tapped controls are the smallest: checklist status toggles at 36px, "Fix" chips at 28px, quick links at 32px. Below 44px, error rates roughly triple for this audience — and add breathing room between destructive and safe actions.

Class 5 · Where The Users Live

Kill the PDF. Win Facebook.

Secretaries promote shows by posting PDF schedules into Facebook groups. The replacement isn't a better PDF — it's a show page whose link looks irresistible in a Facebook feed.

Ofcom's research says older users aren't gullible online — they're cautious. They click links vouched for by people and clubs they trust, with previews that prove what's behind them. An anonymous schedule_final_v3.pdf proves nothing; a rich preview carrying the club's own crest proves everything before anyone clicks.

  • Auto-generate a poster-quality share card for every show — 1200×630, club crest and colours, show name, date, venue, "Entries close 14 June". You already have the HTML→Chrome render pipeline; this is the same trick. Re-render and force Facebook's re-scrape whenever the show is edited, or the old preview haunts every share.
  • One-tap share at the publish moment. When she publishes, hand her a ready-made Facebook post — image, pre-written plain-English text, link — one tap. Don't make her compose anything. Her existing habit becomes Remi's best acquisition channel.
  • Publish class entry counts — numbers, never names. The equestrian platforms proved visible class counts drive entries ("it tells you how many in classes" is the #1 praise for Showing Scene). Counts preserve the pre-judging privacy rule; dogs and owners stay hidden until show day.
  • The club's identity leads; Remi is quiet infrastructure. Older users trust the club they know. The page hero, the share card, the email sender-name — club first, Remi underneath. (This also matches Mandy's marketing guidance.)
  • Readable web address. remishowmanager.co.uk/shows/clyde-valley-gsd-open-2026 is itself a trust signal for a scam-wary audience. Already mostly there — protect it.

The schedule stays downloadable as a PDF for the print-and-post traditionalists — but the page is the product, readable on a 375px screen inside Facebook's own browser, no login wall.

Class 6 · Moments That Matter

Celebrate the right things

Remi already does confetti on show creation. But the most emotional moments in a secretary's year currently pass in silence.

  • The first entry. The single most anxious-then-joyful moment of her show. Today: nothing. It deserves a notification — "Your first entry just arrived: Mrs Carter with Ch. Moonwind Apollo." Highest emotional return of anything in this report, and it's a day's work.
  • Entries closing. Today: a toast. It should be a wrap-up screen: "87 dogs, 142 class entries, £1,204 for the club. Here's what happens next."
  • A judge says yes. Today: a green line in the checklist. It's an event in her world — tell her properly.
  • Show-day morning. A 7am briefing, ready when she wakes: dogs entered, judges, ring open time, the absentee-marking link.
  • After the show. A recap with a shareable results graphic and an honest stat: "Remi saved you roughly nine hours of paperwork this show." That recap is what she shows the committee — and the other clubs.
  • Checklist hits 100%. The number ticks over silently today. Fire the dog confetti: "Your show is fully prepared. Enjoy the day."
  • Quiet reassurance, always. The schedule form autosaves invisibly; for this audience, an always-visible "All changes saved ✓" is not a detail — it's the difference between confidence and re-typing everything into Word "just in case".

Class 7 · Worth Building

The gaps that matter

From a full inventory of every feature in the codebase — what's missing that a world-class UK show platform would have.

📅 Closing-date alerts

Email (and later WhatsApp) reminders: "Entries for shows you follow close this week." Aggregator sites exist purely because the incumbents don't do this. It's an acquisition loop disguised as a feature.

📋 Copy last year's show

Canva's founding insight: never show a blank canvas. A returning club should start from last year's show — classes, judges structure, fees — and edit. Templates make non-designers feel capable; same trick for secretaries.

🏆 Best in Show cascade

Group → BIS progression with automatic eligibility is the heart of the all-breed plan already on file — it's also the showpiece demo that wins all-breed societies. The steward flow has the bones; finish it.

🔁 Transfers & waiting lists

Entries close, life happens. Self-service class transfers and a waitlist for capped shows remove the two most common "email the secretary" chores left.

🧾 A proper receipts page

Exhibitors get a confirmation email and then… nothing to point at. Order history with re-downloadable receipts builds the money-trust this audience needs. (The API surface for it already exists and is tested.)

⚖️ Closing-night confidence

Organisers in adjacent markets explicitly ask whether entry systems "crash when everyone logs on at closing". Load-test the closing-night spike, then say so publicly: "Built for closing night."

Class 8 · The Engine Room

The code review — done, not just described

Michael asked for the cleanup to happen autonomously, so it did — today, in four deployed commits, with the full suite green after each.

✓ Shipped to production · 12 June 2026

Security — found, verified, fixed, deployed

  • Club bank details were publicly readable. Every public show payload embedded the club's full record — including sort code and account number — and one public endpoint served it directly. Any visitor with browser dev-tools could read every active club's bank details. Now allowlisted to safe fields everywhere. This was the most serious finding; it's closed and live.
  • Draft shows were enumerable by anyone — including secretary home addresses and phone numbers, before a show was announced. Closed.
  • Public dog profiles revealed upcoming entries — a judge could see which dogs are entered in their own upcoming show (the exact pre-judging risk Remi's rules exist to prevent), plus unpublished results and critiques. Now owners-only until show day; results appear only once published.
  • A forgotten legacy payment endpoint skipped all entry validation and created entries the club would never have been paid for. Deleted.
  • Cross-club gaps: any secretary could read a rival show's revenue stats, and edit any judge's contact email (intercepting another club's contract offers). Both locked down, with new regression tests.
  • Refund safety: a timed-out-then-retried refund could pay out twice; concurrent refunds could under-record. Both fixed with idempotency keys and atomic updates.
  • Silent email failures: ~20 places ignored the email provider's rejection response — a judge offer could "send" to a dead address while the checklist ticked itself complete. All secretary-initiated sends now surface failures honestly.

Dead weight removed

−4,400 lines

Dead components and endpoints deleted — including a 1,462-line abandoned copy of the public show page, two superseded routers, and 17 orphaned API procedures. Every deletion verified zero-callers first.

137 scripts archived

One-off probes, data fixes and experiments swept out of scripts/ into an archive folder, with a README documenting the ~15 scripts that actually matter.

5 dependencies dropped

Packages with zero imports removed from the build. Smaller installs, fewer security advisories, faster deploys.

1 landmine defused

Three database enums were never exported to the migration tool — the next schema push to production would have failed mid-flight. Found because it broke the test database; fixed for good.

Still on the list (deliberately deferred)

  • Database constraints (a missing foreign key on the money-reconciliation column, three missing uniqueness rules) — needs a coordinated schema push, parked until the working tree's pending changes ship.
  • Splitting the 7,200-line secretary router into domains — the access-control gaps above hid in there because it's too big to review.
  • One shared money-formatter (37 hand-rolled pence conversions across 12 files) and one shared date-formatter (76 raw conversions, including one that can shift a dog's age-eligibility by a day at midnight UTC).
  • Full technical detail, file-by-file, lives in research/code-review-2026-06-12.md in the repo.

Class 9 · Order of Attack

Ninety days to "best in class"

Weeks
1–2

Quick wins, visible immediately

Defuse the status badge · 44px touch-target pass · rewrite errors and empty states to the "not your fault" formula · "All changes saved ✓" · the first-entry celebration. Nothing here needs design debate; it's all execution.

Weeks
3–6

The front door

Three-question show creation feeding the Setup Wizard · one readiness spine · seven doors instead of fourteen · plain-English pass on the critical path · "Check your answers" before publish and before payment. This is the stretch that converts the hesitant majority, not just the brave early adopters.

Weeks
7–12

The growth engine

Auto-generated Facebook share cards + one-tap share at publish · public class counts · closing-date alerts · the post-show recap secretaries will show their committees · "copy last year's show". Meanwhile the engine room work (router split, shared formatters, schema constraints) runs in the background.

One discipline ties it together: once the front door is rebuilt, stop moving the furniture. This audience rewards stability like no other — the platform that never surprises them is the platform they tell their friends about. And in this world, the friends all run clubs too.
